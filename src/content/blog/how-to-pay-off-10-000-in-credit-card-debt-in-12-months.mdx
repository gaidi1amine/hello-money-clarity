You make a payment. A week later you check the balance, and it's barely moved.

That's not a discipline problem. It's a math problem — and once you see the math, the plan for fixing it gets a lot simpler.

If you're carrying $10,000 in credit card debt and want it gone within a year, here's the real number you're working with, the method that gets you there, and how to track it so you're not guessing every month.

## Why Credit Card Debt Doesn't Move Even When You're Paying

Credit card interest is calculated daily, not once a year. Every payment you make first covers the interest that's already piled up. Whatever's left over is what actually reduces your balance.

As of mid-2026, the average interest rate on credit card accounts carrying a balance is a little over 22%, according to [Federal Reserve data](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/). At that rate, a large share of a "normal" monthly payment covers interest, not the balance itself.

This is why minimum payments can feel endless. They're often sized to barely outpace the interest, not to clear the balance in any reasonable timeframe.

If you're not sure which payoff method makes sense for your situation, [Debt Snowball vs. Debt Avalanche: Which Payoff Method Actually Works](/debt-snowball-vs-avalanche) breaks down the difference before you keep reading — the plan below works with either one.

## The Actual Math: What It Takes to Clear $10,000 in 12 Months

Divide $10,000 by 12 and you get $833 a month. That's the number most people start with. It's also wrong, because it ignores interest.

Factor in a 22% APR, and the real monthly payment needed to fully pay off $10,000 in 12 months is closer to **$935 a month**. Over the year, that adds up to roughly $1,230 in interest on top of the original balance.

That gap — $833 versus $935 — is the entire reason this needs a plan instead of a guess. Your exact number will vary based on your card's specific rate. Treat $935 as a planning target, not a precise figure, and check your own statement for the real one.

## Step 1: Pick Your Payoff Method

Two approaches work for this kind of timeline:

* **Debt avalanche** — put extra money toward the highest-interest debt first. This minimizes total interest paid, which matters most when your timeline is short and your rate is high.
* **Debt snowball** — put extra money toward the smallest balance first. This builds momentum through visible wins, which matters if staying motivated for 12 straight months is the harder part for you.

If $10,000 is spread across more than one card, the method you choose determines which one gets the extra payment each month. If it's a single card, the method matters less — the target is just this one balance.

## Step 2: Build Your Manual Payment Log

You don't need software to start. A notebook or basic spreadsheet with four columns is enough:

1. **Date** of the payment
2. **Amount** paid
3. **Balance** after the payment
4. **Interest charged** that cycle (found on your statement)

Update it every time a payment posts. This one habit turns "I think I'm making progress" into "I know exactly where I stand." It's the same habit that makes the 12-month target feel achievable instead of abstract.

### Quick Answer: How Much Do I Need to Pay Monthly to Clear $10k in a Year?

At an average credit card rate of roughly 22% APR, paying off a $10,000 balance in 12 months takes approximately $935 per month — not the $833 you'd get from simple division, because that figure doesn't account for interest.

The manual log above works. It also means recalculating your progress, your interest paid, and your remaining timeline by hand every time you log a payment.

## Who This Plan Isn't For

This plan assumes $10,000 is realistically payable at $900–950 a month, and that no new charges are added to the balance while you pay it down.

If that monthly amount isn't feasible right now, a 12-month timeline isn't the right target yet. A longer payoff window is one option. A lower-interest personal loan or a nonprofit credit counseling service like the [National Foundation for Credit Counseling](https://www.nfcc.org) is another.
