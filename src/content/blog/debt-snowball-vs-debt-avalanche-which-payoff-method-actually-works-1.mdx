Have you ever started paying extra toward one card or loan, then a month later wondered if you picked the wrong one to focus on? That doubt is common — and it usually means you never actually compared the two methods side by side, just picked whichever one the last article you read happened to prefer.

Most people have never seen both methods run against the same numbers, so there's nothing real to compare against. So instead of telling you which one is "better," this post runs both methods against the exact same three debts, so you can see what actually changes — and pick based on what you're likely to stick with, not based on someone else's opinion.

#### Why "which is better" is the wrong question

Both methods are solving for a different failure point, not competing to be the most mathematically efficient:

* **The snowball method solves for motivation collapse.** Most people don't quit debt payoff because the math stopped working — they quit because progress felt invisible for too long.
* **The avalanche method solves for mathematical waste.** It's built to minimize the total interest you pay, full stop, regardless of how the progress feels along the way.

Neither one is wrong. They're built to fix two completely different problems, so the "right" answer depends on which problem you actually have. The average credit card APR currently sits well above 20%, according to [Federal Reserve consumer credit data](#) — which is part of why the method you pick here isn't just a preference, it has a real cost attached either way.

#### What is the debt snowball method?

The debt snowball method means paying minimum payments on every debt, then putting every extra dollar toward the debt with the **smallest balance** — regardless of its interest rate — until it's paid off. Then you roll that payment into the next-smallest balance, and repeat.

**How to order your debts for snowball:**\
Smallest balance first, largest balance last. Interest rate isn't part of the sorting at all.

Using our three example debts:

1. Medical Bill — $1,200
2. Student Loan — $5,000
3. Car Loan — $6,000

**Worked example (snowball):**\
Say the monthly debt budget is $1,500, and the combined minimum payments across all three debts are $1,140 — leaving $360 in extra money each month. Every extra dollar goes to the Medical Bill first.

At that pace, the Medical Bill clears in about 2 months. That freed-up payment then rolls into the Student Loan, which clears around month 9. From there, everything rolls into the Car Loan, which clears around month 11.

**Who this actually fits:** people who've tried to pay off debt before and lost steam, or anyone who needs to *see* a debt hit zero to stay motivated to keep going.

#### What is the debt avalanche method?

The debt avalanche method means paying minimum payments on every debt, then putting every extra dollar toward the debt with the **highest interest rate** — regardless of its balance — until it's paid off. Then you roll that payment into the next-highest rate, and repeat.

**How to order your debts for avalanche:**\
Highest interest rate first, lowest interest rate last. Balance size isn't part of the sorting at all.

Using the same three debts:

1. Car Loan — 20%
2. Medical Bill — 10%
3. Student Loan — 1%

**Worked example (avalanche):**\
Same $1,500 monthly budget, same $360 in extra money. This time, the extra goes to the Car Loan first, since it carries the highest rate.

The Car Loan clears around month 8. Interestingly, the Medical Bill — while not the target — pays itself off around month 4 just from its own minimum payments, since the balance was small to begin with. Once the Car Loan clears, everything rolls into the Student Loan, which finishes around month 11.

**Who this actually fits:** people who are bothered by paying more interest than necessary, even if the visible progress feels slower at first.

#### Snowball vs avalanche: the same debts, side by side

|                             | Debt Snowball                          | Debt Avalanche                                                   |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Order of attack             | Medical Bill → Student Loan → Car Loan | Car Loan → Medical Bill → Student Loan                           |
| Approx. time to debt-free   | ~11 months                             | ~11 months                                                       |
| Approx. total interest paid | ~$739                                  | ~$487                                                            |
| First debt cleared          | Month 2                                | Month 4 (Medical Bill, self-paid) / Month 8 (Car Loan, targeted) |

*Note: these figures are illustrative estimates based on fixed monthly payments, with no changes to rates, balances, or budget over time — your real numbers will vary. This isn't personalized financial advice; check your own lender statements for exact balances and rates.*

In this example, both methods take roughly the same amount of time — but the avalanche method saves about $250 in interest, simply because it attacks the highest-rate balance first. That gap gets bigger the more your interest rates differ from each other, and smaller when your rates are close together.

#### How to decide which one actually fits you

Instead of asking which method is objectively better, ask yourself:

* Have I started a debt payoff plan before and lost momentum partway through?
* Do I check my balance for reassurance, or do I check it to track the math?
* Does paying extra interest genuinely bother me, or does it not really register emotionally?

If you recognized yourself in the first two questions, snowball is probably the one you'll actually finish. If the third question hit harder, avalanche is likely worth the extra patience it asks for early on.
