Ever make a payment and check your balance five minutes later, hoping it moved? That's not a sign you're doing something wrong. Student loans are built in a way that makes progress genuinely hard to see, especially in the first few years. This student loan payoff plan is built around how these loans actually behave, not around a generic "pay more each month" tip.

## Why Most Student Loan Plans Don't Actually Move the Balance

**Interest capitalization** is when unpaid interest gets added to your loan's principal, so future interest is then charged on a bigger number. A payment that only covers part of the interest doesn't touch the principal at all — the balance can hold steady or even grow.

Most people repaying student loans aren't following a plan — they're on autopay, sending the minimum, and hoping the balance shrinks. It often doesn't, and the reason isn't a lack of effort. Capitalization has happened for years on many federal and private loans whenever a borrower switched repayment plans, or came out of deferment or forbearance (a temporary pause on payments).

This isn't the same everywhere. Some newer federal repayment options introduced in 2026 changed how unpaid interest is handled on certain plans, and private loans follow whatever's in the original promissory note (the loan agreement you originally signed). The mechanism is the same everywhere it applies — the plan and loan type change whether it's currently working against you.

## Step 1: List Every Loan Separately, Not as One Number

Federal and private student loans behave differently — different repayment options, different forgiveness eligibility, sometimes different servicers entirely. Lumping them into one total makes it impossible to see which loan is actually doing the damage.

For each loan, write down: the servicer, whether it's federal or private, the balance, the interest rate, and the minimum payment. This takes ten minutes and it's the only step every plan after this one depends on.

## Building a Student Loan Payoff Plan: Choosing Your Order

Once every loan is listed separately, you're choosing between two orders to attack them.

**Snowball** targets the smallest balance first, regardless of interest rate. You keep paying minimums on everything else and put any extra money toward that one loan. It's slower on total interest paid, but the first loan disappears faster — for a lot of people, that early win is what keeps the plan going past month three.

**Avalanche** targets the highest interest rate first, regardless of balance size. It costs less in total interest over time, but the first "win" can take longer to arrive if your highest-rate loan also has a large balance.

Say you're carrying three loans: $4,000 at 6.8%, $9,000 at 5.5%, and $15,000 at 4.5%. Snowball has you targeting the $4,000 loan first — smallest balance, fastest first payoff. Avalanche has you targeting that same $4,000 loan first too, since it also carries the highest rate here. Sometimes the two methods agree, and when they do, the choice is easy. When they don't agree, pick snowball if you need an early win to stay motivated, and avalanche if the interest math genuinely bothers you more than the wait.

## Should an Income-Driven Plan Help or Slow You Down

Federal student loan repayment changed significantly in 2026. A federal court vacated the SAVE plan. Two new plans — the Repayment Assistance Plan and the Tiered Standard Plan — launched July 1, 2026, replacing most older income-driven options for many borrowers. This is genuinely still in transition as of this writing, and the right plan for you depends on things a blog post can't know — when your loans were first disbursed, whether they've been consolidated, and your income.

The trade-off underneath all of this stays the same regardless of which specific plans are currently available: an income-driven option can lower your monthly payment by tying it to what you earn, but it usually stretches out how long you're paying and can increase total interest paid, sometimes with a tax bill on any amount eventually forgiven. A fixed payment plan costs more per month but pays down the loan faster with less total interest.

Because the specifics are changing in real time, check your exact current options directly at studentaid.gov or with your loan servicer before choosing. The [Department of Education's official fact sheet](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/fact-sheet-trump-administration-simplifying-student-loan-repayment) covers the RAP and Tiered Standard basics, but this section will be updated as the transition settles — your servicer's numbers will always be more current than a blog post's.

## A Realistic Timeline, Not a Promised Date

It's tempting to want an exact "debt-free by" date. Don't build your plan around one. Your actual timeline shifts every time your income changes, your rate changes, or a payment gets applied differently than expected — a fixed date promised up front is usually wrong within a few months.

A more reliable habit: check your progress monthly. How much did the balance actually move? Did interest eat most of this month's payment, or did principal actually go down? That monthly check tells you more than any projected end date would.
