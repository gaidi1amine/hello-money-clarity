As of 2026, total household debt in the U.S. averages around $63,500 per person — mortgages, credit cards, student loans, and auto loans combined. If your own balances add up to more than that, you're not behind some invisible line. Most people carrying debt right now are carrying more of it, spread across more categories, than they'd guess.

## Average Debt in 2026, By Type

**Credit cards.** Total U.S. credit card debt passed $1.28 trillion in late 2025, and the average borrower carries around $6,500 in revolving balance — the amount that carries over from month to month instead of being paid off in full each cycle. The real cost isn't the balance itself. It's the APR (the yearly interest rate you're charged), which is sitting near 21% in 2026. A balance that doesn't move month to month is quietly getting more expensive, not staying the same.

**Student loans.** The average federal student loan balance is around $40,500 per borrower as of mid-2026, and more than 42 million Americans are still paying one down. Student loans now make up close to 10% of all household debt nationwide, up from under 5% twenty years ago.

**Auto loans.** Total auto loan debt has climbed past $1.6 trillion. Unlike a credit card, auto debt is secured by the car itself, which changes the risk. It still competes for the same monthly budget as everything else.

**Mortgages.** These dwarf every other category, at over $13 trillion nationally, with an average balance north of $150,000 per mortgage. This is the piece that skews the overall "average debt" number the most.

## Why the Average Doesn't Tell the Whole Story

An average is just one number standing in for millions of very different situations. Someone with a mortgage and no other debt gets averaged in right next to someone with three credit cards and a car loan and no mortgage at all. Both show up in the same statistic, but their actual financial pressure looks nothing alike.

Age matters too. People in their 40s carry the highest total debt loads of any age group. That's usually the decade when a mortgage, an auto loan, and sometimes leftover student debt are all being paid down at the same time. Someone in their late 20s with $15,000 in credit card and student loan debt combined isn't "behind" the $63,500 average — they're just earlier in a different stage of it.

The average tells you where the country stands. It doesn't tell you where you stand, and it was never designed to.

## What Actually Matters More Than the Average

Two numbers predict how debt actually feels — and neither of them is the total balance.

**Interest rate exposure.** A $6,500 credit card balance at 21% APR behaves completely differently from a $40,000 student loan at 5%. The higher-rate debt grows faster and costs more to carry, even though the balance is smaller. This is the entire logic behind paying off high-interest debt first, regardless of what the total number looks like.

**Debt-to-income ratio.** $20,000 in debt against a $90,000 income is a very different situation than $20,000 in debt against a $35,000 income. Lenders use this ratio for a reason — it's a far better predictor of how much breathing room someone actually has than the raw dollar amount.

If you're trying to figure out whether your own debt is "a lot," these two numbers will tell you more in five minutes than any national average will.
